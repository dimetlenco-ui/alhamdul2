Development Economics · Research Proposal

Mobile Money as Informal Insurance: Rainfall Shocks and Consumption Smoothing in Rural Uganda

Short research project · 2026

Research question

When a rainfall shock hits, do rural households that use mobile money smooth consumption better than those that don’t — and is it because they receive more remittances from their network?

Motivation

Most rural households in Sub-Saharan Africa have no formal insurance. They cope with bad harvests by drawing on family and friends, but sending money across distance used to be slow and costly. Mobile money cuts that cost sharply. In Kenya, Jack and Suri (2014) found that households using M-Pesa kept their consumption steady after negative shocks, while non-users saw it drop by about 7%. This project asks whether the same holds in Uganda, where mobile money spread later and through a different market structure, and it uses weather shocks that households cannot influence instead of self-reported ones.

Hypotheses

  1. H1: A drought lowers per-capita consumption less for mobile money users than for non-users.
  2. H2: Users receive more remittances in shock years, and remittances account for much of the difference in H1.
  3. H3: The buffering effect is largest for households far from a bank branch, where the alternatives are worst.

Empirical strategy

A household fixed-effects panel regression, following Townsend (1994) and Jack and Suri (2014):

ln Cit = αi + δrt + β1Shockit + β2MMit + β3(Shockit × MMit) + Xit′γ + εit

Here C is per-capita consumption, αi are household fixed effects, δrt are region-by-year fixed effects, and MM indicates mobile money use. The coefficient of interest is β3. If mobile money helps, it should be positive and offset part of the negative β1.

Identification concern: households choose whether to use mobile money, and users tend to be richer and better connected. Three responses:

  • Use distance to the nearest agent in place of, or as an instrument for, individual use.
  • Control for baseline wealth and education interacted with the shock.
  • Run a placebo test: future adoption should not predict how households coped with past shocks.

For H2, re-estimate the model with remittances received as the outcome. Standard errors are clustered at the parish level.

Expected contribution

New evidence from outside Kenya on whether mobile money works as informal insurance, using weather shocks that are plausibly exogenous, and a direct test of whether remittances are the channel. The findings speak to policy on agent-network expansion and on mobile money taxes, which Uganda introduced in 2018.

Limitations

  • Mobile money use is self-reported and measured only in some survey waves.
  • Agent placement is not random, since operators put agents in busier areas.
  • Rainfall captures only covariate shocks. Idiosyncratic shocks such as illness are tested separately using the survey’s shocks module.
